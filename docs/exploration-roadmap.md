# Exploration authoring roadmap

**Status:** Working roadmap based on Keivan's requirements of 5 October 2026. The reference-naming convention is awaiting his choice.

An exploration follows the significant steps through which a question, explanation, or solution develops. An easily formulated problem should be stated directly; the exploration can focus on finding its solution. It can reach a complete result or leave a clearly identified question open. This standard guides new explorations and revisions.

## Core requirements

- Observe, guess, and examine significant ideas and obstacles. Each example, experiment, calculation, and argument has a distinct purpose. Returning to an example should reveal something new.
- Explore formulation when it adds insight. State straightforward problems and routine assumptions directly; explain the motivation behind substantial definitions, choices, and reformulations.
- Remove repetition and routine detail while retaining motivation, key insights, and non-obvious inferences.
- Distinguish observations, conjectures, numerical evidence, informal explanations, and proofs. State relevant assumptions and edge cases; a finite experiment cannot establish a general claim by itself.
- Include tables, data, statistics, code, and technical digressions only when they advance understanding.
- Let the topic determine length, background, and structure. Introduce unfamiliar prerequisites briefly; no sections, experiments, or extensions are mandatory.
- Apply minimality, meaningfulness, alignment, appearance, and consistency to the writing and visuals together.

## Shape the investigation

When the problem is easy to formulate, begin with its statement and assumptions. Concentrate the exploration on the useful attempts, obstacles, and insights through which a solution emerges. When the question itself needs investigation, begin with a configuration, surprising pattern, or apparent obstruction and develop the formulation as its significant features become clear.

Let each step explain the need for the next. Test a particular guess, compare alternatives, or expose a limitation. Include a failed attempt when it motivates another approach; avoid artificial suspense and an exhaustive diary of trial and error.

Make the goal and assumptions explicit at the appropriate point. Develop the argument around its key insight, exploring the significant steps rather than every detail. Keep the main reasoning visible and readable without operating a widget. A supplementary derivation or extension can have its own named place when it would interrupt that path.

End by interpreting what was established and what remains uncertain. Include a further question only when it follows naturally; avoid repeating the article.

## Names and references

- Give necessary figures, widgets, and cited formulas stable anchors and concise, descriptive identities. Introduce them near their first use and link later references directly to them.
- Use one consistent naming convention within the article. The choice between a shared Figure sequence, separate Figure and Experiment sequences, or primarily descriptive titles is pending Keivan's decision.
- Number formulas only when cited later, using (1), (2), and so on. Define symbols before use and keep notation consistent between prose and visuals.
- Put article reference labels in titles or captions, clear of mathematical point labels, move numbers, and controls.
- Keep captions brief: identify the visual and explain how to read it. Interpret it in the argument without duplicating that explanation in subtitles and Info panels.
- Keep descriptive anchors stable when numbering changes. Numbers belong to the article; a reusable widget can have a different reference label elsewhere.

## Figures and interactions

Follow the [widget design roadmap](widget-requirements.md): shared frames, controls, panels, typesetting, and numeric formatting. Static figures share its typography, colours, line treatment, notation, and spacing. Reuse components and styles across explorations, lessons, and standalone puzzles.

Use the [exploration logo roadmap](logo-roadmap.md) for each exploration's identifying SVG mark. A diagram, formula, or simple combination follows the same visual standard; the Magician's problem logo is the approved reference.

Identify the question each visual helps answer. Use interaction when changing, comparing, or tracing adds insight; otherwise a static figure may suffice. A brief invitation to explore the widget, change settings, and consult Info is usually sufficient. Leave instructions for operating its controls in the widget itself. Add a mathematical prompt only when it advances the investigation, and connect useful observations to the argument. Keep essential results visible.

Use appropriate scales and the shared precision rule, retaining extra digits when they reveal the phenomenon, as in the magician's probability ripple. Distinguish calculated values, simulated frequencies, and approximations. Provide a useful static view or textual explanation when interaction is unavailable.

## Development sequence

1. **Establish the standard.** Settle the reference convention and link this roadmap from the writing guide.
2. **Map an investigation.** Identify which parts of the question or solution need exploration. Sketch the significant attempts, observations, and key argument; develop the formulation only where useful. Identify each visual's contribution and remove repeated or routine steps.
3. **Apply to a representative article.** Keivan selected Four equal regions on 5 October 2026. Following his feedback, the local article states the problem directly and explores two bisections, the remaining imbalance, and a quarter-turn proof. It reuses the existing puzzle with a brief invitation, a descriptive anchor, and two cited equations. Refine the conventions before extending them to other articles.
4. **Support reusable references.** Add only the shared anchor, title, or caption support the article needs, preserving each activity's shared presentation. Update authoring examples to match.
5. **Review together.** Check mathematics, experiment behaviour, references, and notation. Remove duplicate explanations. Inspect text, formulas, figures, and controls at desktop and phone widths, including keyboard use and the static reading path.

Ask Keivan when a choice needs his opinion, present concrete alternatives, and record the decision here. Resolve routine choices using established standards.

## Review criteria

- The problem and assumptions are clear; exploration concentrates on significant steps in the formulation or solution, as the topic needs.
- Every return to an example, every visual, and every section advances the investigation.
- Evidence and proof are distinguishable; the argument is correct and complete for the claims made.
- References are useful and resolve correctly; symbols, labels, units, and precision agree throughout.
- The article remains concise and readable, and its visuals fit the website's shared style.
