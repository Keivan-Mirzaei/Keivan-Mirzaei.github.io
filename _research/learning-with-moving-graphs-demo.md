## A teaching question

Does asking students to predict a graph before changing a parameter help them explain the relationship afterward?

This example shows how a teaching or education project could be presented alongside mathematical research. The linked lessons are working demonstrations. No classroom study, participant data, or measured learning gains are claimed.

## The activity sequence

Each lesson follows four steps: predict, manipulate, explain, and transfer. The graph gives immediate feedback, but students must still explain what the feedback means.

In the parabola lesson, a student predicts a horizontal shift, tests the prediction, then works backward from a vertex and an extra point to an equation. The tangent lesson distinguishes a limit from an undefined quotient at zero. The probability lesson makes the changing denominator visible through counts.

## A possible evaluation

Use short questions before and after a lesson to examine explanations, not only numerical answers. Include a new example that does not reuse the activity’s exact values.

Record whether students can state which quantity changes, which stays fixed, and why. Any actual classroom study would need its own documented design and appropriate consent process before data collection.

## Materials and access

The activities use keyboard-operable native controls. Text feedback accompanies each graph; the probability activity includes a table. Explanations and worked answers remain readable without scripting.
